The best programs I use are the ones I stop thinking about. They start the same
way every time, they fail in ways I can predict, and they never ask me to hold
their hand. I open them, do the thing, and close them again. That absence — the
not-thinking — is the whole point, and it is surprisingly hard to design for.

## Absence is a feature

We tend to measure software by what it does: features, integrations, the length
of the changelog. But a tool earns its place in your day by what it lets you
*not* do. A good editor doesn't remind you it exists. A good backup runs while
you forget it's running.

> The goal isn't to be used. The goal is to be relied on, quietly.

## Designing for the exit

If you want people to leave your software alone, make the common path boring and
the rare path obvious. Boring is a compliment here — it means predictable, and
predictable means trustworthy. Every surprise is a small tax on attention, and
attention is the one resource your users can't buy more of.

So I try to ask a different question when I build: not *what else could this do*,
but *what can I remove so the person using it can think about something else?*
